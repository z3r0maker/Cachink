Xangarro Postgres: scale and performance audit

Round 2 of N-27 · 2026-09-26 · branch feat/landing-mostrador @ 4c1c837b · baseline: docs/audits/db-2026-09-17.md

Verdict. The schema's foundation is right for a multi-tenant SaaS. It uses one shared schema with RLS, and every tenant table has a business_id-leading index. Money is stored as bigint centavos. The per-tenant sync cursor is correct. Pull is one cheap ordered stream: 0.8 ms per 5,000 entries on a whale. You do not need a new database design to reach 5,000 tenants.

What will fail is four code paths, and all of them were measured failing on a disposable copy of the real schema:

  1. A usage recount after every push does a full-table scan across all tenants. Its cost grows with the whole database, not the tenant.
  2. The push is written row by row. A 500-row evening backlog takes 8.0 s and holds a connection and the tenant's lock the whole time. Batched, the same push takes 68 ms.
  3. Pushes of more than 64 rows overflow Postgres's subtransaction cache. That slows every session in the cluster, not just the pushing one.
  4. Portal reads grow with a tenant's history. For example, Inicio's daily series takes 8.1 s on a one-year heavy tenant. That is past the production 5 s timeout, so the page errors.

Each has a measured fix, 20–100× faster, that stays inside the current architecture. Storage is the second issue. At 5,000 realistic tenants you add about 28 GB a month, which trips ADR-068's S2 trigger in the first month. 43% of that is sync_receipts, which can be pruned.

9. Method and limits

1. Load model

The plan limits are 300 / 10,000 / 30,000 transactions a month (Xangarrito / Xangarro / Xangarrote, packages/domain/src/entities/plan.ts). They are advisory (ADR-065), so 30K is the top plan's cap, not a hard ceiling. One "transaction" is one ticket. Measured on the benchmark data, a ticket produces:

  • 1 tickets row
  • 1.6 sales lines
  • 1.1 stock movements
  • 0.1 expenses
  • 3.8 sync_receipts (one per pushed row)
  • 1.1 sync_log entries (movements are HYBRID, so every other phone pulls them)

That is about 8.7 rows and 2.7 KB per ticket. "Realistic" assumes 60% of tenants on Xangarrito (300 a month), 30% on Xangarro at 3,000 a month, and 10% on Xangarrote at 10,000 a month.

Peak assumption: 70% of a day's pushed rows arrive between 17:00 and 20:00, and the busiest 15 minutes run at 3× that window's average. Storage is linear in rows, so these figures are direct extrapolations of measured bytes per row. The 5,000 × 30K row is a ceiling, not a forecast: it would be about 190 tickets a day for every tenant.

2. The 17:00–20:00 peak, measured

I replayed POST /api/v1/sync/push with the repo's own postgres.js 3.4.9, using the exact statement sequence of ApplyPushUseCase + PgPushStore:

  • the device-route auth transaction, the throttle and the latency counter
  • then, per row: the reference checks, the receipt lookup, a SAVEPOINT, the upsert, the cursor bump, the sync_log entry and the receipt

Each statement waits a simulated 1 ms round trip, the Vercel pdx1 ↔ Supavisor ↔ Postgres hop. The 15 clients match about what Supabase Nano's pooler gives the app role. The server had 4 vCPU and 4 GB, and the production role timeouts were set (5 s statement, 10 s idle in transaction).

How to read this for your peak:

  • Raw volume is not what breaks at 5,000 realistic tenants. About 260 rows/s is within today's 890–1,117 rows/s.
  • The shape of the peak is. At closing time a shop's two or three phones reconnect together and flush the day's backlog. That is exactly the same-tenant contention row: today's code serialises them behind the tenant's sync_cursors lock and hits the 5 s timeout, and each timeout becomes a retryable INTERNAL row. That feeds a device-side bug that leaves those rows stuck (DB2-DEV-01).
  • The per-push usage recount rides on top. It is a full-table scan per push, on a second connection pool (DB2-USE-01).
  • At the 30K plan cap (~3,700 rows/s), only the batched push has headroom.

Profiling the batched run with pg_stat_statements put xangarro.api_latency_record at 65.8% of all statement time, with a mean of 30 ms. That is mostly lock wait: every call from every tenant increments the same few (day, endpoint, bucket) rows (DB2-HOT-01).

4. Findings

New IDs are DB2-*. Carried-over items from the first audit are marked. Severity reflects the 5,000-tenant target. Each finding lists its evidence, the fix, and the effort.

Critical: breaks the evening peak or a core screen

criticalDB2-USE-01The usage recount after every push does a full scan of every tenant's movements
Where
apps/web/src/app/api/v1/sync/push/route.ts:37 → refreshUsageAfterPush (server/usage/live.ts:58) → xangarro.usage_counts (drizzle/0035_usage_counts_tickets.sql)
Evidence
  • The function filters on created_at, but the only index is (business_id, fecha). The planner picks a Parallel Seq Scan on inventory_movements (885 ms) even for a 16K-row tenant.
  • Measured per call: 0.60 s (mid), 0.89 s (heavy), 1.19 s (whale). The cost is set by the size of the whole database, and it runs after every accepted push, including one per sale while a phone is online.
  • With (business_id, created_at) indexes it drops to 17 / 30 / 148 ms.
  • The nightly all-tenant run takes 6–7 s at 1,003 tenants and grows with the total row count.
  • saveUsageCounters (queries/metering.ts:61) writes one INSERT with 5 parameters per counter row. It breaks postgres.js's 65,534-parameter limit at about 6,550 tenants.
Fix
  1. Now: add (business_id, created_at) on sales, expenses and inventory_movements, created CONCURRENTLY.
  2. Proper: increment usage_counters inside the push transaction (ON CONFLICT … SET transactions = transactions + n). Keep the nightly recount as reconciliation only.
  3. Chunk saveUsageCounters into batches of 1,000.
Effort
Index: S. Incremental counter: M (touches the push store and the domain rule "one ticket = one transaction").
criticalDB2-SYNC-01The push runs about 8 sequential statements per row, all inside the tenant's lock
Where
packages/application/src/apply-push/apply-push-use-case.ts:55-90, apps/web/src/server/sync/pg-push-store.ts, upsert-row.ts, packages/data-pg/src/sync/cursor.ts:20
Evidence
  • One sale is 54 statements. A 500-row push is 4,212, and the old audit's recommended batching (DB-SYNC-05) was not done.
  • A 500-row push takes 8.0 s alone (68 ms batched) and 7.2 s p50 under load. It holds a pooled connection and the tenant's sync_cursors row lock for all of it.
  • Backlog throughput: 1,117 rows/s today vs 32,578 batched.
  • Same-tenant contention: 67 statement timeouts a minute today, 0 batched.
  • A 3 ms round trip cuts live throughput by 2.6×. Today's design is bound by round trips, not by the database.
Fix

Batch per table in one transaction:

  1. Check references with one = ANY($ids) per referenced table.
  2. Look up receipts with JOIN unnest($tables, $ids) on (table_name, row_id). My first batched attempt left out table_name, missed the primary key and burned 86% of CPU; that is a trap to test against.
  3. Upsert each table with one multi-row INSERT … ON CONFLICT … RETURNING id.
  4. Take the seqs with one cursor bump, last_seq = last_seq + n RETURNING, and assign them in order (ADR-078 already anticipates "allocate a block of seqs per batch").
  5. Write sync_log and receipts with one bulk insert each.
  6. If the batch fails, bisect the batch or fall back to row by row, capped at 64 rows.

Keep the response contract, one outcome per row.

Effort
M–L. Needs the C-10 conformance suite and an old→new test. This is the single highest-leverage change.
criticalDB2-SYNC-02Pushes of more than 64 rows overflow the subtransaction cache, slowing every session
Evidence
  • postgres.js 3.4.9 savepoint() never sends RELEASE (node_modules/postgres/src/index.js:258-281). Even released, a savepoint that wrote keeps its subtransaction XID until commit.
  • Postgres caches 64 of these per backend. During the backlog run, pg_stat_get_backend_subxact showed 15 of 15 app backends overflowed.
  • Once a backend overflows, every snapshot in the cluster, for every tenant, has to consult pg_subtrans. This is a well-known cliff (SLRU contention) that shows up as load-dependent slowdowns nobody can attribute.
Fix
Comes free with DB2-SYNC-01, because there are no per-row savepoints. Until then, cap MAX_PUSH_DELTAS at 64. That also shortens lock holds.
Effort
S as a stopgap (one constant in the contract, device side too).
criticalDB2-QRY-01Inicio's daily series makes 60 full-history scans and times out for big tenants
Where
packages/data-pg/src/queries/serie.ts:23, called on every Inicio load (apps/web/src/server/inicio.ts:52)
Evidence
It makes 30 days × 2 correlated subqueries, each with left(fecha,10) = day, which cannot use the index. That is 8,094 ms on the whale, against the app role's 5 s statement_timeout in production, so Inicio errors for a year-old heavy tenant. It is 107 ms rewritten.
Fix
One GROUP BY fecha with fecha BETWEEN $desde AND $hasta, plus the ticket-cancel anti-join, and a UNION with expenses. Later, move it to a daily rollup.
Effort
S.

High: fails or degrades before 1,000 tenants

highDB2-DEV-01Retried rows can be stranded as pending forever, which blocks cierre
Where
packages/sync/src/push.ts:103 (markPending before sending), status-store.ts:119 (dueRetries selects only status='rejected')
Evidence
  • When a batch that carries retry rows fails as a whole (5xx, 429, network, timeout), the slice rows are re-read from the high-water mark. The retry rows stay pending and nothing ever reselects them.
  • On the caja, cierre requires pendientes === 0, so it stays blocked permanently.
  • A slice of 401–500 rows plus up to 100 retries also exceeds MAX_PUSH_DELTAS=500, so the server answers 400 and the same stranding happens.
  • The evening peak manufactures exactly these failures.
Fix
On a whole-batch failure, return the sent retry rows to rejected with their old retryAfter, or have dueRetries also pick up stale pending rows. Size the slice as 500 − retries.length. Add a unit test for both cases.
Effort
S.
highDB2-HOT-01api_latency_counters is one hot row shared by every tenant
Evidence
  • Every push, pull, entitlement and comprobante call runs INSERT … ON CONFLICT (day, endpoint, bucket_ms) DO UPDATE hits = hits + 1 (drizzle/0042_api_latency.sql). Traffic concentrates on about 3 rows a day.
  • Measured: 65.8% of statement time, 30 ms mean, mostly lock wait.
  • It is fire-and-forget (observability/latency.ts:21) but still takes a pooled connection while it waits.
Fix
Add a slot smallint (random 0–15) to the key and sum the slots on read. Or aggregate in process and flush once a minute. Or send it to the logs pipeline instead of Postgres.
Effort
S.
highDB2-QRY-02Lists and exports load a tenant's entire history (carries DB-QRY-02)
Evidence
  • listVentas returns 576K rows in 3.6 s on the whale, with a correlated sync_receipts→devices lookup per row.
  • listGastos, listProductos, listRejections, listNotices, ventasPorProducto / fiadoPorCliente (server/revision.ts:111,150) and all exports are unbounded. Exports are built fully in memory in a Vercel function.
Fix
Use keyset pagination on (fecha DESC, id DESC) with a partial index (business_id, fecha DESC, id DESC) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL. Default to the current month. Read the device from the row's own device_id, not from receipts. Stream exports by cursor.
Effort
M (screens need a "cargar más" or a date filter).
highDB2-QRY-03RLS trap: a join placed before LIMIT makes the planner walk the whole history
Evidence
  • With the right index, a 50-row keyset page on sales alone takes 0.1 ms. The same page joined to tickets takes 1,395–2,649 ms.
  • RLS predicates give the planner no per-tenant row estimate. So it drives from tickets, nested-loops 420K rows, re-parses the JWT JSON as a per-row filter on the unwrapped sales policy, then sorts 567K rows to keep 50.
  • Neither an explicit business_id predicate (2.1 s) nor wrapped policies (1.3 s) fixes it.
  • Paginating sales in a subquery and then joining takes 0.5 ms.
Fix
Make it a coding rule, with a lint or code-review checklist item: in tenant queries, ORDER BY … LIMIT the driving table in a subquery first, then join the lookups.
Effort
S per query.
highDB2-QRY-04left(fecha,10) defeats the (business_id, fecha) index everywhere (supersedes the "fixed" DB-QRY-01)
Evidence
  • Used by periodLedger, balance.ts (enRango), serieDiaria and the Asesor windows.
  • One month on the whale: 280 ms, against 48 ms sargable. The cost grows with every month of history.
  • N-27's board says DB-QRY-01 is fixed. It is not: the filter moved into SQL but still reads every row the tenant has.
Fix
Normalise any timestamped fecha once (data migration), add CHECK (fecha ~ '^\d{4}-\d{2}-\d{2}$') NOT VALID then VALIDATE, and use plain fecha BETWEEN. Aggregate the statements in SQL instead of shipping rows to JavaScript.
Effort
S–M.
highDB2-QRY-05Stock is summed from all movements ever, in 7 places, and the bootstrap ships them all
Evidence
  • The seven places: lowStock, listProductos, stockACosto, the Asesor, sumStock, the product export and the bootstrap.
  • inventory_movements has no producto_id index.
  • Whale: 350–374 ms (114 ms with a covering index).
  • The bootstrap sends 396K movement rows for the whale in one response, while Vercel caps bodies at 4.5 MB.
Fix
Add a product_stock(business_id, producto_id, stock, updated_at) rollup, maintained in the same transaction as every movement write (push, portal, cancellation, conversion). Reconcile it nightly. Bootstrap from the snapshot plus the last N days of movements. As an interim step, add the covering index (business_id, producto_id) INCLUDE (tipo, cantidad, deleted_at).
Effort
M (a new table on the Postgres side only; the device keeps deriving its own stock).
highDB2-SYNC-03sync_receipts is 43% of the database and nothing prunes it or sync_log
Evidence
  • 3.54 GB of 8.2 GB, the largest table: 12 M rows against 5 M sales. It has one row per pushed row, forever.
  • Its long-term readers are only the per-row device lookup (movimientos.ts:61) and historialSync. The row's own device_id column already answers the first.
  • sync_log keeps one entry per stocked sale line, also forever.
Fix
  1. Stop reading receipts for "which device": use device_id.
  2. Keep receipts for the idempotent re-push window only, for example 90 days past devices.acknowledged_through. Range-partition them by received_at month and drop old partitions, which is free and generates no vacuum work.
  3. Record each device's pull cursor server-side (devices.pulled_through). Prune sync_log below the minimum across active devices and older than N days. A device behind that horizon gets a RESYNC and re-bootstraps.
Effort
M. Needs an ADR, since it amends ADR-078's retention assumptions.
highDB2-CONN-01Connection budget: two pools per instance, no idle timeout, and pull waits on the second pool inside a transaction
Evidence
  • createDb uses max: 5 and no idle_timeout (packages/data-pg/src/client.ts:23). There is one pool for the app and another for metering on each warm instance.
  • Supabase Nano gives the app role about 15 pooled server connections (check the dashboard). docs/ops/provisioning.md:220 itself suggests 1–2 per instance.
  • Pull awaits usageFor() on the metering pool while its app transaction is open (server/sync/pull.ts:38). If DB2-USE-01 saturates the metering pool, pulls hold app connections and risk the 10 s idle_in_transaction kill.
  • Each device call also opens 3 separate checkouts before its real work: the auth transaction, the throttle and the latency write.
Fix
Set max: 2 and idle_timeout: 20 per instance behind Supavisor. Read usage before opening the tenant transaction, or inside it on the same connection. Fold the revoked-device check into the tenant transaction.
Effort
S.
highDB2-OPS-01No backups, no PITR, no restore drill (carries DB-OPS-01 / O-3)
Evidence
docs/ops/provisioning.md:47-51; the project is on Nano with "No backups". The cursor-rewind procedure the old audit asked for is unwritten.
Fix
Move to a paid plan with PITR and run the drill in the 2026-09-17 audit §5 before the first paying customer. It matters more now, because a restore that rewinds sync_cursors must force re-bootstrap on devices holding higher cursors.
Effort
Owner action + S.
highDB2-MIG-01The migration pipeline can't ship the fixes above safely
Evidence
  • drizzle/meta/_journal.json has 8 entries, and its snapshots stop at 0007: 35 tables against 45 in the schema. The next db:generate would re-emit existing objects.
  • The hosted runner wraps every file in a transaction, so CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY is impossible. At 5,000 tenants a plain index build on sales blocks writes for minutes: this run built 6 indexes in 41 s on 5 M-row tables.
  • Ten duplicated numeric prefixes. Two late additions (0033_geo_prune, 0023_business_branding) sort before files that were applied earlier, so hosted and fresh databases apply them in different orders.
  • NOT VALID/VALIDATE is never used.
Fix
This is the never-created task B-20:
  1. Rebuild the drizzle snapshot, or declare drizzle-kit generate retired.
  2. Support non-transactional migration files, for example with a -- no-transaction header, so CONCURRENTLY can run.
  3. Refuse pending files that sort below the last applied file.
  4. Add a migration lint: lock_timeout, CONCURRENTLY on large tables, NOT VALID constraints.
Effort
M. A prerequisite for every index in this report once real data exists.

Medium: needed on the way to 5,000

mediumDB2-IDX-01Index set: what to add and what to drop

Add (each CONCURRENTLY, after DB2-MIG-01):

  • sales, expenses, inventory_movements: (business_id, created_at). For usage (DB2-USE-01) and recent activity.
  • inventory_movements: (business_id, producto_id) INCLUDE (tipo, cantidad, deleted_at). For stock and sumStock.
  • sales: (business_id, fecha DESC, id DESC) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL. For keyset lists.
  • tickets, expenses: (business_id, caja_turno_id). For /cortes, which today scans all tickets 3× per request.
  • tickets: (business_id, cliente_id). sales: (business_id, producto_id). For revisión merges and fiado.
  • sync_receipts: (business_id, received_at), or partition by it (DB2-SYNC-03).
  • business_members (user_id) + unique (business_id, user_id). The login path, carried from DB-IDX-02.
  • portal_sessions (business_id, last_seen_at). For the console's last login.

Drop (each duplicates its primary key or a prefix of it, and adds write cost):

  • sync_receipts_business_idx (79 MB on the largest, hottest table)
  • sync_cursors_business_idx
  • billing_customers_business_idx
  • business_logos_business_idx
  • business_onboarding_business_idx
  • notice_preferences_business_idx
  • usage_counters_business_idx

Rewrite idx_sales_ticket and idx_client_payments_cliente as business_id-leading, per ADR-053 §7's shard rule. The drop list is safe because the tenant-indexes test is satisfied by the primary key.

mediumDB2-DEV-02Client behaviour at the peak: synchronised retries, and the caja pulls after every sale
  • The 15-minute tick, the online flush and the row backoff have no jitter.
  • Retry-After is ignored (api-client.ts:60), and there is no fetch timeout.
  • A whole-batch failure retries on the next trigger with no backoff.
  • The caja PWA runs push and pull on every sale, with no debounce, and the cashier waits (operador/shell/cola.tsx).
  • A reconnect flushes up to 10 × 500 rows back to back.

Fix: add ±20% jitter, honour Retry-After, and use exponential backoff with full jitter on batch failure. The caja should push per sale but pull at most once every 30–60 s. Effort: S.

mediumDB2-CRON-01Nightly and console jobs scan everything and grow with the database
  • The usage cron at 03:00 does full scans (see DB2-USE-01).
  • The digest's over-limit check recomputes usage from source rows for up to 1,000 tenants, then reports partial above that. It should read usage_counters.
  • xangarro.admin_user_email compares u.id::text = p_user_id, which defeats the auth.users primary key: one sequential scan per owner on the tenants list.
  • security_prune() only runs manually, so portal_sessions and throttle grow forever.
  • The CFDI close runs 2 sequential primary-key queries per payment in one invocation.

Fix: read the counters, cast the parameter instead of the column, and schedule the prune. Keep all of these in the 00:00–06:00 trough (see §8). Effort: S.

mediumDB2-PAGE-01Every portal navigation costs about 8 transactions, one of them a full count(*) of sales
  • The session is UPDATEd on every request.
  • loadChecklistSignals runs count(*) FROM sales for the whole tenant on every page (layout guiaPrimero) and again on Inicio.
  • loadShellCounts runs twice on Inicio.
  • negociosOf runs one transaction per membership.

Fix: use EXISTS instead of count(*), persist "checklist complete", wrap loaders in React cache(), and throttle the session UPDATE to once a minute. Effort: S.

mediumDB2-CHK-01Integrity is enforced only in application code (carries DB-CHK-01/02, DB-FK-01, DB-TYPE-02)
  • 41 of 45 enum columns have no CHECK.
  • There is no cantidad > 0, no fecha format check, and no non-negative money check.
  • business_members (business_id, user_id) is not unique.
  • There are no foreign keys on the ledger, and the two foreign keys that were added use ON DELETE CASCADE.
  • The proposed task B-19 was never created.

The push path validates with Zod, but portal actions, imports, the admin console and seeds do not all go through it. Fix: generate the CHECKs from the same as const arrays, add them NOT VALID then VALIDATE, and add an equality test so the two cannot drift. Effort: M.

mediumDB2-KEY-01Keys are 26-character text under en_US.utf8 collation
  • Every id, business_id, device_id and foreign-key column is collation-aware text. The primary key indexes average about 60 bytes per row.
  • On 3 M ids, COLLATE "C" sorted in 1.4 s vs 2.8 s. That was on this Alpine/musl image; glibc hosts such as Supabase usually show a larger gap.
  • ULIDs are uppercase Crockford base32, so C order equals the intended time order. Nothing changes semantically.

Fix: first check the database collation on the host with SELECT datcollate FROM pg_database. If it is not C/C.UTF-8, declare COLLATE "C" on key columns when their indexes are next rebuilt, or at the S2 partitioning migration. Do not change the id type; the device mints ULIDs. Effort: M, done opportunistically.

Low and decisions

lowDB2-RLS-01Wrapped vs unwrapped policies: measured, and it barely matters (downgrades DB-RLS-02)

29 of 40 tenant policies still call current_business_id() unwrapped, including sales, sync_log and sync_receipts. On indexed access the whale's count(*) was 240 ms unwrapped and 272 ms wrapped, which is noise. It only helps plans that mix tables (DB2-QRY-03 went from 2.6 s to 1.3 s, but the real fix there is the query shape). Wrap them all in one migration, for consistency and cheaper sequential scans, but not urgently.

lowDB2-PART-01Partitioning for ADR-068 S2: decide the key before you need it

At 5,000 realistic tenants, S2 triggers in month 1. My recommendation, to be settled in an ADR:

  • Sync bookkeeping (sync_receipts, and sync_log once its horizon exists): range by time, so retention is DROP PARTITION.
  • Ledger tables (sales, tickets, inventory_movements, expenses): hash by business_id, 16–32 partitions. Every query is tenant-scoped, so pruning is automatic, per-partition vacuum and index builds get small, and it is the same distribution key Citus uses for S3.

Month partitions on fecha would help reports but not tenant scoping. fecha is also device-supplied and text.

The prerequisite either way: the primary key must include the partition key. (business_id, id) means push's ON CONFLICT (id) becomes ON CONFLICT (business_id, id). That is cheap to change now, inside DB2-SYNC-01, and expensive later.

5. Normalization and structure

The schema reads as a ledger. That is the right model for a NIF system, and most of its apparent denormalization is correct. Intentional and correct:

  • sales.concepto, categoria and monto are copied from the product at capture time. A ledger must not change when the catalogue does.
  • sales.fecha is copied from its ticket, which lets it be filtered without a join.
  • caja_turnos.total_* and efectivo_esperado are a closing snapshot.
  • Receivables are derived from facts (ADR-074), not stored.
  • Stock is derived from movements. That is correct as the source of truth, but it needs a server-side rollup for reads (DB2-QRY-05).

Real defects:

  1. entregas_credito.sale_ids is a text list (breaks first normal form). The server can't join, index or constrain it. If the portal ever needs "which delivery contained this sale", use a join table (business_id, entrega_id, sale_id).
  2. Cancellation is stored twice. tickets.cancel_motivo, cancelled_by_user_id and cancelled_at duplicate cancelacion_logs. Queries read tickets.cancelled_at (the NOT EXISTS anti-join in every revenue query), so decide which one is authoritative and derive the other. As an aside, a boolean or partial index on cancelled tickets would let revenue queries skip the per-line anti-join.
  3. Enum lists are repeated. The payment method appears in tickets, client_payments and cancelacion_logs; the expense category in expenses and recurring_expenses. There is also no database CHECK. Generate one CHECK per column from one as const (DB2-CHK-01).

Acceptable, and should stay as it is:

  • JSON stored as text (feature_flags, permissions, atributos*, lineas, denominaciones) is fine for device parity. Move a column to jsonb only when the server starts querying inside it.
  • businesses.business_id duplicating id is a side effect of the shared audit columns. It is harmless and keeps RLS uniform.

8. Load curve and the Azure note

The traffic has one daily peak and one daily trough:

  • Peak, 17:00–20:00 CDMX. Phones reach home Wi-Fi, flush the day's outbox and pull. Portal traffic (owners checking the day) lands in the same window.
  • Trough, 00:00–06:00.

Today's scheduled jobs already sit mostly in the trough: usage at 03:00, the CFDI close at 01:00 on the 1st, trial emails at 09:00 and the digest at 08:00. The Asesor fan-out, once it is built, must be scheduled there too.

Scaling compute up for the peak only pays off after DB2-SYNC-01. Until then the peak is bound by round trips (see the 3 ms row in §2), and a bigger SKU does not help.

I added the note you asked for as owner row O-29 in docs/plan/11-pre-launch-and-deferred.md, and regenerated PENDIENTES.md, where it now appears. It covers:

  1. Keep batch work in the trough.
  2. Scale down overnight and up before 17:00, only if the p95 (ADR-068's 800 ms trigger) and CPU call for it. A compute change restarts the server, so schedule it, and check whether near-zero-downtime scaling is available on the chosen SKU.
  3. Add a read replica before any vertical step.
  4. Compare against reserved capacity, which makes a scale-down worthless.
  5. Storage only grows; size it separately.
  6. Use the built-in PgBouncer in transaction mode.
  7. Precondition: batch the push first.

I used the id O-29, not O-6c: the board parser only accepts O-<digits>. For the same reason the existing O-6b Azure note has never appeared on the board.

9. Method and limits

Environment. A disposable postgres:17-alpine container, xangarro-audit-scale, port 55498, capped at 4 vCPU / 4 GB (shared_buffers=1GB). All 53 real migrations were applied with db-local.sh apply. The shared xangarro-pg* containers were not touched.

One incident to report. My first load filled the Docker VM's 58 GB disk for about a minute. I removed my container at once, which freed 21.5 GB, and checked that all eight other Postgres containers still accepted connections. The reload used a 1 GB WAL cap.

Data. 1,003 synthetic tenants: 600 × 300, 300 × 3,000, 100 × 10,000 for one month, plus 3 whales × 30,000 a month for 12 months. That is 3.16 M tickets, 5.06 M sale lines, 3.48 M movements, 316K expenses, 12.0 M receipts, 3.48 M sync_log rows and 8.2 GB. It is 1:5 on tenant count. Storage and row counts extrapolate linearly. Per-tenant query cost depends on the tenant's own history (the whale), except where a query scans whole tables (DB2-USE-01), which is why that one is critical.

Queries. Run as xangarro_app with request.jwt.claims set, EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS), warm cache, second run.

Push. A Node replay with the repo's postgres.js 3.4.9 (prepare:false, as production), a 1 ms simulated round trip per statement, and production role timeouts. Throughput varied ±10% between identical runs.

Not verified.

  • Hosted Supavisor overhead, TLS, and Supabase disk I/O and compute. Absolute numbers will differ; the before/after ratios hold.
  • Real traffic in pg_stat_statements: there is still none.
  • The subtransaction-overflow damage to other sessions. I observed the overflow, but my reader comparison was confounded by CPU load, so no number is claimed.
  • Vercel cold starts and the real 4.5 MB body limit behaviour.
  • Azure pricing and SKU behaviour, which is written as items to check.

The benchmark scripts (push.mjs, generators, harness) are in the session scratchpad next to this file.
